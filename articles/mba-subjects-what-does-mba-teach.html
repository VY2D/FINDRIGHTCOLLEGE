MBA Subjects — What Do You Study in an MBA, and What Does It Teach You?

An MBA teaches you how a business runs: marketing, finance, human resources, operations, strategy and data analysis. The first year covers the core subjects managers need, and the second year lets you specialise — Finance, Marketing, HR, Business Analytics or Operations. You do not just memorise subjects; you learn to decide with incomplete information, which is the skill of management.

If you are considering an MBA, you have probably wondered what you will actually study for two years — and whether it is all theory, or something you can use. The honest answer is: it is both, and the mix is the point.

Year 1 — the core subjects everyone studies

The first year is common across specialisations. Subject names vary slightly by university, but the core is the same everywhere:

  • Marketing Management. How customers decide, how brands are built, and how pricing and distribution work.
  • Financial Management. How money moves in a business: budgets, costs, profits, investment decisions, and reading a balance sheet without fear.
  • Organisational Behaviour and Human Resource Management. How people work in teams, why employees stay or leave, and how to lead without authority.
  • Operations Management. How products and services are produced and delivered — supply chains, quality, logistics, and the systems behind a business that looks simple from the outside.
  • Business Economics and Business Environment. How markets, government policy, inflation and competition affect a company — and why good companies fail in bad times.
  • Business Communication and Business Analytics. How to present, negotiate and write clearly, and how to read data and turn it into a decision. Analytics is now a core subject, because every modern manager works with data.
  • Strategic Management (usually in year 2, but introduced early). How a company chooses where to compete and how to win.

Most programmes also include business law, entrepreneurship, and a project or internship where you apply the subjects to a real business problem.

Year 2 — specialisation subjects

In the second year you choose a specialisation, and your subjects change to match it:

  • Finance: Investment Analysis, Corporate Finance, Financial Markets, Risk Management, Taxation — for students who like numbers and want roles in banking and finance.
  • Marketing: Consumer Behaviour, Brand Management, Digital Marketing, Sales Management, Market Research — for students who like people, creativity and understanding why customers buy.
  • Human Resources (HR): Talent Acquisition, Performance Management, Labour Laws, Training and Development — for students who like working with people and building teams.
  • Business Analytics: Data Visualisation, Predictive Analytics, Big Data, Machine Learning for Business — for students who like data and want roles in analytics.
  • Operations and Supply Chain: Logistics Management, Supply Chain Analytics, Quality Management — for students who like systems and planning.
  • International Business, Healthcare, Entrepreneurship and more — for students with a specific industry in mind.

What an MBA actually teaches you (beyond the syllabus)

The subjects are only half the education. An MBA also teaches four things that no single subject covers:

  • Decision-making with incomplete information. In case studies and projects, you rarely have all the data. You learn to decide anyway, defend your decision, and live with the result.
  • Working with people you did not choose. Group projects force you to work with different personalities, just like a real office.
  • Presenting and persuading. You present cases, defend your analysis, and learn to make a point in five minutes instead of fifty.
  • Seeing the whole business. A finance person who understands marketing makes better decisions than a specialist who only sees one corner. The MBA's real value is this whole-picture view.

How many subjects are there in an MBA?

There is no single fixed number, because every university divides its syllabus differently — but a typical 2-year MBA has around 20–30 subjects in total, spread across 4 semesters, plus projects and practical work. The first year usually has 5–6 subjects per semester; the second year has fewer classroom subjects and more specialisation work, projects and internships. Do not choose a university by the number of subjects — choose it by the specialisations offered and the total fee. Frequently Asked Questions

What do you study in an MBA?

In the first year you study Marketing Management, Financial Management, Organisational Behaviour, Human Resource Management, Operations Management, Business Economics, Business Communication and Business Analytics. In the second year you specialise — Finance, Marketing, HR, Business Analytics or Operations — and study advanced subjects in that area, plus projects and internships.

What does an MBA teach you?

An MBA teaches you how a business runs — marketing, finance, people, operations and strategy — and how to make decisions with incomplete information. Beyond the syllabus, it teaches you to work in teams, present and persuade, and see the whole business instead of one department.

How many subjects are there in an MBA?

A typical 2-year MBA has around 20–30 subjects across 4 semesters, plus projects and practical work. The exact number varies by university. The first year has more classroom subjects; the second year has more specialisation subjects, projects and internships.

Which MBA specialisation is best?

There is no single best specialisation — it depends on the work you enjoy. Finance suits students who like numbers and banking; Marketing suits students who like people and creativity; HR suits students who like building teams; Business Analytics suits students who like data and technology. Choose by the daily work you can enjoy for years, not by what sounds most impressive.

Ask Your Senior

If you are worried that an MBA is "too much theory", here is the truth: the theory is the easy part. The hard part — and the valuable part — is learning to decide, to lead people, and to see a whole business clearly. Pick the specialisation that matches work you actually enjoy, not the one your relatives praise most. And if you are doing the MBA online while working, use your own job as your classroom: apply every subject to a real problem at work.
